# Choosing and Managing Models

I am open to new technology, but I want to understand what it brings to the business. Before we start a trial, agree on what better looks like and how we will measure it. A convincing demonstration is useful. Evidence that we can improve the work at an acceptable cost gives us a reason to invest.

During a divestiture, we needed to establish an independent technology environment. We moved enterprise workloads to the cloud while keeping factory systems local because of operational technology security and specialized requirements. The business circumstances drove the move, and the needs of the workloads shaped the approach.

Getting applications into the cloud did not automatically make them efficient there. Some retained their traditional client-server designs, and we did not always have the knowledge needed to take advantage of more cloud-native approaches. Costs stayed high where we carried those patterns forward without making the necessary adjustments. Strong FinOps practices helped us run workloads more economically. Cloud can be expensive, but our experience showed that we could run workloads more cheaply when we actively managed how we used it.

Self-service automation took considerable work, but developers could receive the infrastructure they needed in minutes instead of weeks. That gave them more time to build and less time waiting for us. I believe enabling developers to build and get code out the door faster helps the company innovate faster. When that work delivers a product or capability customers will pay for, getting it into their hands sooner gives us the opportunity to generate revenue sooner.

That is the kind of connection I want to see when we evaluate AI. Show me where it removes a delay, improves the work, or helps us deliver something valuable. Then account for the effort required to make that improvement dependable. Selecting a capable model is part of the decision. The business outcome depends on what we build around it.

## Start With the Work We Want to Improve

Someone may arrive with a preferred model or a service they have been using personally. That experience can be useful. We still need to establish what we are asking it to do for the company.

"We need an AI assistant" describes a proposed solution. It does not explain what is taking too long, where mistakes occur, or what the organization hopes to improve. A useful starting point is a business task with an identifiable outcome.

Suppose employees spend time reviewing service requests and routing them to the right team. We could evaluate whether a model helps classify those requests, identify missing information, and suggest a destination. That gives us something specific to assess.

Before introducing the model, understand how the work happens today. Establish the volume, handling time, common errors, and effort required to correct them. Include the people doing the work. A process diagram may show five clean steps while the person handling requests can explain the twelve other things that happen between them.

The baseline does not need to be perfect. It needs to be credible enough that we can recognize an improvement. If we cannot explain how we will measure success, we are likely to finish the trial with several enthusiastic opinions and very little basis for a decision.

Business outcomes can take different forms. We might reduce turnaround time, improve consistency, handle more work with the existing team, or avoid an expense. Some applications may help generate revenue. The important part is connecting the proposed capability to a result the business actually values.

## Establish the Approved Options

Before sending company information to a service, we need to work within the organization's approval process. That may involve an AI Center of Excellence, or AI CoE, along with the functions responsible for strategy, governance, security, risk, and compliance.

An existing approved offering should give teams a straightforward starting point. A new provider or a use outside the approved boundaries needs the appropriate review. We should also check whether something we already own can meet the requirement. Another subscription should bring enough value to justify another service we have to manage.

Vendor evaluation and model evaluation are connected, but they answer different questions. The vendor review establishes whether the service and its terms are acceptable for the proposed use. The model evaluation establishes whether a particular model can perform the task well enough.

A provider can pass our review and still offer a model that struggles with our documents. A model can perform impressively while the service around it fails to meet our requirements. We need both decisions to hold.

The data-handling answers need to apply to the offering we intend to buy. Understand whether our inputs or outputs may be used for training, what is retained, who can access it, and which settings or contractual terms govern those practices. An answer about a provider's consumer product does not necessarily describe its enterprise service, or the other way around.

Built-in guardrails deserve the same attention. Establish what the provider supplies, what our administrators can configure, and what our application must enforce. We covered those responsibilities in Part II. Here, they help define which options are suitable for evaluation before performance becomes the deciding factor.

## Test With Work That Looks Like Ours

A trial should use representative work within approved data boundaries. Include the normal cases, the difficult cases, and the incomplete requests that someone will inevitably submit on a Friday afternoon.

For the service-request example, a collection of neatly written requests would tell us only part of the story. We should also include ambiguous descriptions, missing details, unfamiliar terminology, and requests that could reasonably belong to more than one team.

Agree on what a useful result looks like before comparing models. Correct routing may matter most, but the system should also recognize when the information is insufficient. A confident guess can create more work than a clear request for clarification.

The people who understand the task should help assess the results. A technically valid response may still be useless to the team receiving it. Conversely, a plain answer that saves someone several minutes may be more valuable than a beautifully written explanation.

Keep the comparison reasonably consistent. Use the same task definitions, representative inputs, and assessment criteria. If one candidate receives better instructions or more relevant information, we need to account for that before attributing the difference to the model.

Record failures as well as successes. We need to know what kinds of work require intervention and whether those failures are acceptable for the intended role. A model that performs well as a drafting assistant may be unsuitable for making the final decision.

We will examine evaluation methods in more depth later in the guide. For this decision, the trial should give us enough evidence to select an option, narrow the proposed use, or stop. It should have a review point instead of becoming a permanent competition to see which provider wins next week's demonstration.

## Choose for the Task and Its Consequences

Model selection involves tradeoffs. Quality matters, but so do response time, cost, supported inputs, and the effort required to integrate the result into the workflow.

A model helping someone interpret a complicated document may justify a longer wait if the result is substantially more useful. A model supporting an interactive task may need to respond quickly enough that people keep using it. The acceptable balance depends on the work.

The consequences of an error matter too. A suggested category that a person can quickly correct presents a different decision from an output that triggers an operational action. We should evaluate the model for the role it will actually have, including the review and controls around it.

More capability does not automatically produce more business value. A narrow extraction task may be served adequately by an option that would struggle with a broad analytical assignment. We should give each candidate a fair opportunity to demonstrate whether it meets the requirement.

The lowest advertised price does not settle the decision either. If a cheaper option requires repeated attempts and extensive correction, its cost per useful result may be higher. A more expensive option may earn its place if it reliably reduces that extra work.

I would also question whether the task needs a model at all. Some work is already handled well by a rule, a query, or a conventional automation. We should be willing to keep those approaches when they serve the business. Using AI is not the outcome we are measuring.

## Measure the Improvement After Review and Correction

Time saved is useful, but we need to measure the whole task. Generating a response in seconds tells us little if someone then spends ten minutes checking and repairing it.

For our service-request trial, measure the effort from receiving the request through getting it to the right destination. Include review, correction, and avoidable handoffs. Track quality alongside time so that a faster process does not conceal more mistakes.

The results may differ by type of request. Straightforward cases may improve substantially while difficult ones still need the same human attention. That can be a worthwhile outcome. It may suggest using the model for a defined portion of the work and preserving the existing path for the rest.

Time saved and money saved are different claims. If employees spend fewer hours on routing requests, the organization gains capacity. That capacity becomes more valuable when it allows the team to handle additional demand, reduce a backlog, or spend more time on work that was being neglected.

In the cloud example, infrastructure delivered in minutes removed a delay for developers. It did not mean every hour between the old request date and delivery date had been an hour of active labor. The improvement was in how soon teams could move forward. That distinction matters when explaining the value.

A financial saving needs a defensible connection to an expense reduced or avoided. We should not present every saved minute as money returned to the budget.

The same care applies to revenue claims. Faster work may help the company deliver sooner or serve more customers, but the connection needs evidence. A useful contribution does not have to carry a dollar figure we cannot explain.

## Put ROI in Context

A basic return-on-investment calculation compares the financial benefit with the full cost over the same period:

ROI = (financial benefit - total cost) / total cost x 100%

The calculation is straightforward. Deciding what belongs in it requires judgment.

Costs can include licenses, usage, integration, supporting infrastructure, security work, administration, and ongoing human review. Initial implementation costs and recurring operating costs should both be visible. An inexpensive model call can sit inside an expensive service.

Benefits should be just as explicit. Separate measured results from estimates, and explain the assumptions behind any projected savings or additional revenue. Do not count the same benefit twice by treating hours saved as both reduced labor expense and additional productive capacity unless there is a clear basis for doing so.

Quality and service improvements may deserve their own measures. Fewer incorrect handoffs or a shorter backlog can matter even when we cannot responsibly convert them into dollars. The business can consider those outcomes alongside the financial case.

Early results will have uncertainty. A small trial can demonstrate promise without proving the economics of a company-wide rollout. We can use that evidence to approve a bounded next step rather than pretending the first estimate settled everything.

The later FinOps chapter will examine the cost of useful work in more depth. For model selection, we need enough financial understanding to decide whether the improvement justifies further investment.

## Know What Surrounds the Model

A model does not perform enterprise work in isolation. Its results depend on the instructions, information, tools, and application around it.

When a trial fails, replacing the model should not be our only response. The application may have supplied outdated information, omitted necessary context, or asked for an output the receiving system cannot use. Those problems deserve investigation before we conclude that the model lacks the required capability.

The opposite is true as well. Additional instructions and supporting components may improve results, but they introduce work and cost of their own. We should evaluate the combined service we would actually operate.

This also affects where the model runs. A managed service and a model we host ourselves carry different responsibilities. Hosting gives us responsibility for the serving environment, capacity, and maintenance. Managed access still leaves us responsible for our application, its use of data, and its dependencies.

Licensing and usage terms belong in the decision regardless of deployment approach. Being able to download model files does not, by itself, establish that every intended use is permitted or that operating the model will be inexpensive.

That brings me back to the cloud migration. Access to a platform created an opportunity. Engineering, automation, and financial discipline determined how much value we could get from it. We should expect to do that work around AI as well.

We will examine gateways and model serving in the next two chapters. At this stage, the selection should include enough understanding of the surrounding service to avoid approving a model while overlooking the work required to use it.

## Make a Decision We Can Explain

At the end of the trial, I want a decision that the team and the business owner can understand. We should be able to explain the task, the improvement we observed, the remaining limitations, and the cost of putting the capability to work.

Sometimes the evidence will support moving forward. Sometimes it will support a narrower use, further testing, or stopping. Finding out that a model does not improve the work is a useful result if we act on it before making a larger commitment.

The selected model does not need to win every comparison. It needs to perform its assigned role well enough, within the approved boundaries, at a cost the business can justify.

Record the choice and the evidence behind it, along with the model identifier, relevant configuration, intended use, and known limitations. The people operating the application should be able to understand what was selected and why without reconstructing the decision from a chat history.

That gives us a reason for the choice beyond a product announcement or a compelling demonstration. It also gives the business a clear view of what it is funding and what improvement it should expect.
